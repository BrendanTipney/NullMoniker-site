Hardware : Input Design

Split Keyboard

A 42-key split ergonomic keyboard with a trackpad built into the right half, so a pointer is always under the thumb and the hands never leave the keys. 01 : Problem

Why build one at all

I write code and design games for a living, which means a keyboard and a pointing device are the two objects I touch most in a day. Every commercial option asks you to accept its opinions about where your hands go. Building my own meant those opinions became decisions I had to justify.

The real motivation goes here, in your words. What was actually wrong with the setup you had? Discomfort, reach, the constant hand trip to the mouse, plain curiosity? This paragraph is the one a hiring manager reads to decide whether you design for a reason or for fun. Both are valid answers, but say which.

The specific target was a pointer you never have to reach for. On a conventional desk the hand leaves the home row hundreds of times an hour to find a mouse and come back. That round trip is the problem I set out to remove.

02 : Constraints

What the design had to survive

Constraints came from three places: my hands, the manufacturing process available to me, and the firmware. Naming them up front is what turned this from a parts order into a design problem.

01

42 keys, no more

A 3x6 plus 3-key thumb cluster per half. Everything a full keyboard does has to be reached through layers instead of surface area, which makes keymap design the real interface work.

02

220mm build plate

Every part had to print on an Anycubic Kobra Neo in 1.75mm filament. That capped the footprint, forced the case to split into printable pieces, and ruled out overhangs that would need support on a visible surface.

03

Wireless power budget

ZMK runs the halves over BLE, so every added component is a draw on a small cell. A trackpad polling continuously is a very different battery story from one that sleeps.

04

Thumb reach envelope

The trackpad had to sit inside the arc the thumb already travels for the cluster keys. If it needed a stretch, it would have failed for the same reason a mouse does.

05

Serviceable, not sealed

Hand-soldered wiring in a printed shell fails eventually. The case had to open without destroying itself and the electronics had to come out as a unit.

06

Usable while learning it

A layout I could not type on would get abandoned inside a week. The migration path mattered as much as the end state.

03 : Prior Art

What I ruled out, and why

Split ergonomic keyboards are a well-explored space. Ruling out the existing answers was faster than designing from nothing, and it forced me to name what I actually wanted.

Off the shelf ergo
Which ones did you look at? Moonlander, Glove80, Kinesis, Advantage, ZSA Voyager? One line each on why it did not fit. Naming real products and rejecting them for real reasons is the single most credible thing on this page.
Keyboard plus trackball
A separate pointing device solves precision but keeps the round trip. It moves the problem a few centimetres closer instead of removing it.
Larger split, 60 keys plus
More keys means less layer discipline, but it also means a wider reach envelope and a case too big for the build plate. The constraint that looked most painful turned out to be the one worth keeping.
Corne plus TPS43
A proven 42-key skeleton with an open firmware target, extended with a trackpad module in the thumb path. Known-good foundation, and the novel part is where the design effort actually goes.

Iteration 03

Placing the trackpad

The hard part of the whole build. The TPS43 has to sit where the thumb naturally rests without stealing a key position or forcing the wrist to rotate. Angle, height, and distance from the cluster are three variables that only resolve by printing them.

How many mount versions did you print, and what changed between them? If you have the rejected prints, photograph them side by side. A row of failed mounts is the strongest image on this page.

05 : Decisions

The calls worth defending

A trackpad, not a trackball or nub

A trackball is more precise and needs no calibration, a pointing stick takes almost no space. I went with a capacitive pad because it supports gestures and scroll without adding a mechanical part that collects dust and eventually seizes.

Capacitive sensing costs power, needs tuning, and is worse than a ball for fine selection work. Confirm this reasoning is actually yours, and add whatever the real deciding factor was.

Building on the Corne rather than designing a layout from scratch

A custom matrix would have let me tune every key position to my own hand. Starting from an established 42-key skeleton meant the switch plate, the firmware target, and a decade of other people's keymaps were already solved, so the design effort could go entirely into the part nobody had solved for me.

I inherited someone else's column stagger, which is a compromise for an average hand rather than mine.

Printed in place of machined or laser cut

FDM in the room beats a better process three weeks away. Being able to test a mount in the evening and print the revision the same night is what made five iterations affordable at all.

Layer lines, weaker screw bosses, and a finish that reads as prototype rather than product. Did you do any post-processing? Sanding, filler, paint, heat-set inserts? Worth saying either way.

06 : Failures

What broke

The most useful section on this page, and the one most portfolios leave out.

List three or four things that genuinely went wrong. Prints that warped or fit wrong, a solder joint that kept failing, the trackpad ghosting or drifting, battery life you did not expect, a layer decision that made you slower. For each one: what you thought would happen, what happened, what you changed. This is the section that separates a designer from a hobbyist, so give it real material rather than a polite version.

07 : Outcome

Where it landed

It is the keyboard I use every day to build games, which is the only test that was ever going to matter.

Close this out honestly. What is measurably better, what is still worse than a normal keyboard, and what would you change if you started again? A case study that ends with "and it was perfect" is the least believable ending available.
