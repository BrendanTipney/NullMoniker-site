A split keyboard with 36 keys and a trackpad in the right half, built around one idea: every letter and every hotkey within reach of one hand, so the other can stay on the mouse. The first build took six months of spare time in fall 2023, and I have been refining the design ever since.

01 : Problem

Why build one at all

I love the puzzle of control interfaces, and the standard keyboard is a poor answer to it. Its layout is stuck in time. I wanted to challenge myself to come up with the best layout I could, in the smallest footprint possible.

The biggest slowdown on a computer is the trip between mouse and keyboard. Most ergonomic keyboards try to solve that by keeping you on the keyboard. That works for coding. It falls apart in CAD, video editing, and any workflow that jumps between programs, where the mouse is doing half the work.

In that kind of work you are rarely typing a whole word. You type the first few letters of a command, or a hotkey. So I flipped the goal: keep one hand on the mouse, and put the whole keyboard under the other one.

That set the requirements. Every key has to be pressable without shifting the hand. Common tasks live on the home row. Every letter can be typed with one hand. And every hotkey has to work from that same hand, whether it is Shift plus a key or Ctrl plus Shift plus a key.

02 : Constraints

What the design had to survive

Constraints came from three places: my hands, the manufacturing process available to me, and the firmware. Naming them up front is what turned this from a parts order into a design problem.

01

36 keys, no more

Five columns and three thumb keys per half. I broke the outer columns off the Corne PCB. Everything a full keyboard does has to be reached through layers instead of surface area, which makes keymap design the real interface work.

02

Tenting that holds

The real physical problem was tenting, and keeping the halves steady once tented. The legs fold flat into the printed base, swing out to set the angle, and have to stop the half from rocking while I type on it.

03

Wireless power budget

ZMK runs the halves over BLE, so every added component is a draw on a small cell. A trackpad polling continuously is a very different battery story from one that sleeps.

04

Room for a trackpad

The TPS43 had to fit into the right half without taking a key position, wire straight to the controller, and sleep with the keyboard so it does not drain the battery.

05

Serviceable, not sealed

Hand-soldered wiring in a printed shell fails eventually. The case had to open without destroying itself and the electronics had to come out as a unit.

06

Usable while learning it

A layout I could not type on would get abandoned inside a week. The migration path mattered as much as the end state.

03 : Prior Art

What I ruled out, and why

Split ergonomic keyboards are a well-explored space. Ruling out the existing answers was faster than designing from nothing, and it forced me to name what I actually wanted.

Off the shelf ergo
Expensive, and none of them had the form factor I was looking for. They are built to keep you on the keyboard, not to work beside a mouse.
Keyboard plus trackball
A separate pointing device solves precision but keeps the round trip. It moves the problem a few centimetres closer instead of removing it.
Larger split, 60 keys plus
More keys means less layer discipline, but every extra column moves keys away from the home row and grows the footprint, which works against the whole point. Cutting keys turned out to be the constraint worth keeping.
Corne plus TPS43
A proven Corne skeleton, cut down to 36 keys, with an open firmware target, extended with a trackpad set into the right half. Known-good foundation, and the novel part is where the design effort actually goes.

04  /  Wiring and controller

Firmware

Electronics and firmware

Switches, diodes, controller, and the trackpad on its own bus. ZMK handles the split link and the keymap; getting a pointing device to behave alongside it is where the configuration work lives.

Each half runs a Nice!Nano v2. The TPS43 is soldered straight to the right Nano's pads on a six wire ribbon and talks over I2C. I moved to a fork of the Azoteq driver that ties the trackpad into ZMK's sleep states, because the original kept the pad awake and drained the right half's battery. A small custom input processor adds laptop style tap and tap to drag.

Anything that took a long time to get working? Worth a sentence if so.

05  /  Keymap layers

Layout

Designing the layout

36 keys means every symbol, number, and modifier lives on a layer. This is interface design with the same rules as any other: frequency drives placement, and anything you have to think about is in the wrong spot.

The letters start from Colemak, then I moved a few of them. Before moving any letter I checked why Colemak put it there, so a change would not undo something it was doing well. The moves put the most important hotkeys back where QWERTY has them. Z, X, C and V sit exactly where every program expects undo, cut, copy and paste, and S is back under the ring finger for save.

Common edits are home row combos. S and R together is Backspace, R and T is Delete, and S and T is Tab, so fixing a typo never means reaching. The S and R backspace is one of the best ideas in the whole layout.

Holding R or T mirrors the right half onto the left hand, so with the right hand on the mouse the left can still type any letter. The thumbs carry Shift, Ctrl, Alt and Win, which covers every modifier combination from the same hand.

Which layer decisions did you make and then reverse after living with them? A keymap you revised because daily use proved you wrong is real user testing with a sample size of one, and it reads that way to a designer.

05 : Software

Learning it with the NullKeys overlay

Fourteen layers and two dozen combos are a lot to hold in your head. The NullKeys overlay is a desktop app I built in Godot that sits on top of everything and shows what the keyboard is doing as I use it: which keys are pressed, which layers are active, and what every key does right now. Hover a key and it explains that key on every layer, plus every combo it belongs to.

The hard part is that a computer only ever receives keycodes. Layers, holds and combos never reach it, and holding A for the Num / Sym layer sends nothing at all. So instead of guessing from keystrokes, a small ZMK module streams the physical key positions and the layer state straight from the firmware.

Keyboard

Firmware module

Listens for key position and layer events and sends 32 byte reports over Raw HID. It only streams while the overlay is asking for data, so the radio stays quiet otherwise.

Bridge

Python and hidapi

Godot cannot open HID devices, so a small Python process reads the reports and forwards them as JSON over local UDP. The overlay starts and stops it automatically.

Overlay

Godot app

Parses corne.keymap directly, so it always matches the firmware, then resolves layers, hold taps and combos and draws the result. Overlay mode draws the keys as outlines for screen recordings.

06 : Decisions

The calls worth defending

A trackpad, not a trackball or nub

A trackball is more precise and needs no calibration, a pointing stick takes almost no space. I went with a capacitive pad because it supports gestures and scroll without adding a mechanical part that collects dust and eventually seizes.

Capacitive sensing costs power, needs tuning, and is worse than a ball for fine selection work. Confirm this reasoning is actually yours, and add whatever the real deciding factor was.

Building on the Corne rather than designing a layout from scratch

A custom matrix would have let me tune every key position to my own hand. Starting from an established Corne skeleton meant the switch plate, the firmware target, and a decade of other people's keymaps were already solved, so the design effort could go entirely into the part nobody had solved for me.

I inherited someone else's column stagger, which is a compromise for an average hand rather than mine.

Printed in place of machined or laser cut

FDM in the room beats a better process three weeks away. Being able to test a mount in the evening and print the revision the same night is what made five iterations affordable at all.

Layer lines, weaker screw bosses, and a finish that reads as prototype rather than product. Did you do any post-processing? Sanding, filler, paint, heat-set inserts? Worth saying either way.

07 : Failures

What broke

The most useful section on this page, and the one most portfolios leave out.

List three or four things that genuinely went wrong. Prints that warped or fit wrong, a solder joint that kept failing, the trackpad ghosting or drifting, battery life you did not expect, a layer decision that made you slower. For each one: what you thought would happen, what happened, what you changed. This is the section that separates a designer from a hobbyist, so give it real material rather than a polite version.

08 : Outcome

Where it landed

It is the keyboard I use every day to build games, which is the only test that was ever going to matter.

Close this out honestly. What is measurably better, what is still worse than a normal keyboard, and what would you change if you started again? A case study that ends with "and it was perfect" is the least believable ending available.
